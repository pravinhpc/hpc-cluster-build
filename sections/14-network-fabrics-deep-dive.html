Network Fabrics Deep-Dive: InfiniBand, RoCEv2, Slingshot, and Omni-Path

The Networking & Fabric section earlier in this guide introduced RDMA and the two mainstream fabric choices — InfiniBand and RoCEv2 — at an intro level. This page goes one layer deeper: a side-by-side technical comparison of the four fabrics an HPC or AI cluster builder is actually likely to evaluate today — NVIDIA InfiniBand (NDR/XDR), RoCEv2 at scale, HPE Cray Slingshot, and Cornelis Networks' Omni-Path Express — plus how each behaves under real NCCL collective-communication traffic, and a worked example estimating all-reduce completion time across fabric choices for a fixed GPU count.

InfiniBand NDR and XDR

NVIDIA's Quantum-2 platform is the seventh generation of InfiniBand architecture and delivers NDR at 400 Gb/s per port: a QM9700-class switch packs 64 NDR ports into 1U for an aggregate bidirectional throughput of 51.2 Tb/s. The generation above it, Quantum-X800 (XDR), doubles per-port speed to 800 Gb/s across 144 ports (72 OSFP cages) using 200 Gb/s-per-lane SerDes, with sub-100 ns port-to-port latency, and a single two-tier fat-tree can connect up to 10,368 ConnectX-8 NICs.

Three mechanisms make InfiniBand distinctive at scale:

  • Subnet Manager (SM) scaling — a single logical Subnet Manager (OpenSM, or NVIDIA UFM for production fleets) computes routing tables for the entire fabric. As fabrics grow into the tens of thousands of ports, UFM's incremental/partial-rebuild routing and telemetry become essential to keep topology-change convergence fast; a naive full SM re-sweep on a huge fabric is measured in seconds, which matters when a link flaps mid-job.
  • Adaptive Routing (AR) — switches make per-packet or per-flow forwarding decisions based on real-time port load rather than a static hash, spreading traffic away from hot spots without operator intervention.
  • SHARP in-network compute — NVIDIA's Scalable Hierarchical Aggregation and Reduction Protocol performs reduction operations (the arithmetic inside an all-reduce) inside the switch ASICs themselves, rather than only at the endpoints. Quantum-X800's third/fourth-generation SHARP delivers 14.4 TFLOPS of in-network computing, which can roughly halve the data volume — and the completion time — of a large collective compared to a pure endpoint-based reduction.

RoCEv2 at Scale

RoCEv2 carries RDMA over standard Ethernet/UDP framing, so it can run on commodity switch silicon — but Ethernet was designed to drop packets under congestion, which RDMA cannot tolerate well. Making RoCEv2 lossless at scale requires two cooperating mechanisms, and each has a well-documented failure mode:

  • Priority Flow Control (PFC) pauses an entire priority class on a link when a switch's ingress buffer nears full. Because PFC pauses the whole class rather than a single flow, it can cause head-of-line blocking (unrelated flows sharing that priority queue stall behind the congested one) and, in topologies with cyclic buffer dependencies, a full PFC deadlock — several switches waiting on each other to drain buffers that never drain, freezing traffic permanently until intervention.
  • ECN + DCQCN is the fix layered on top: switches mark packets with Explicit Congestion Notification as queues approach (but don't yet reach) the PFC threshold, the receiver echoes a Congestion Notification Packet back to the sender, and the sender's rate is quantized down before PFC ever has to fire. Getting this right is a tuning exercise — the well-known ordering rule is xON < ECN-min < ECN-max < xOFF on the switch buffer thresholds — and getting it wrong (thresholds too aggressive, or too loose) reintroduces the exact head-of-line blocking and unfairness problems DCQCN exists to prevent.

NVIDIA's Spectrum-X platform (Spectrum-4 switches + BlueField-3/ConnectX-7/8 NICs) packages a pre-validated combination of PFC, ECN, and adaptive load balancing specifically to avoid hand-tuning this from scratch.

Slingshot (HPE Cray)

Slingshot is HPE Cray's eighth-generation interconnect, built around the Rosetta switch ASIC — 64 ports at 200 Gb/s per direction, using 56 Gb/s PAM-4 SerDes lanes — and it is Ethernet-interoperable at the physical/link layer while adding HPC-specific control-plane features on top. Two design choices set it apart from RoCEv2/PFC-based Ethernet:

  • Fine-grained adaptive routing with global congestion awareness — Rosetta switches evaluate multiple minimal and non-minimal paths in real time (particularly effective in Dragonfly topologies, Slingshot's most common deployment topology) and steer around congestion rather than following a fixed hash.
  • Per-flow congestion control instead of class-based pause — rather than PFC's blunt "pause this whole priority class," Slingshot tracks congestion state for thousands of individual flows and throttles only the specific flows contributing to a bottleneck, which is the architectural answer to the head-of-line-blocking problem that PFC-based Ethernet has to fight with careful ECN tuning instead.

Slingshot deployments are concentrated in leadership-class HPC systems (e.g., Perlmutter, Frontier) and increasingly in HPE Cray AI supercomputer offerings, rather than in general commercial cloud AI infrastructure.

Omni-Path Express (Cornelis Networks)

Omni-Path Express is Cornelis Networks' revival and modernization of Intel's original Omni-Path architecture. The current CN5000 generation delivers 400 Gb/s per port (QSFP112), sub-1 microsecond MPI latency, and a message rate around 1.2 billion messages/second. It supports classic full/partial bisection-bandwidth fat-trees as well as Dragonfly and Megafly (Dragonfly+) topologies, and Cornelis states the architecture is designed to scale to roughly 330,000 nodes in a single cluster. Distinguishing features include dynamic adapter-driven routing and a design goal of "zero-latency" packet-integrity protection (forward error correction and retransmission engineered not to add pipeline latency). Cornelis also publishes an UltraEthernet-aligned roadmap, positioning Omni-Path Express as a bridge between classic HPC-fabric engineering and the emerging Ultra Ethernet Consortium standard.

Fabric Comparison — Relative Positioning

NCCL / Collective Performance Characteristics

All four fabrics can carry NCCL traffic via RDMA transports, but they behave differently under the specific traffic pattern of an all-reduce (ring or double-binary-tree exchange of large gradient tensors). InfiniBand's performance is generally reported as highly consistent run-to-run, because the lossless credit-based link layer removes retransmission jitter from the picture entirely; SHARP further reduces the effective data volume nodes have to move by performing partial reductions inside the switch fabric. RoCEv2 performance is more sensitive to fabric configuration quality — buffer tuning, ECN threshold placement, and background traffic mix all show up directly in NCCL all-reduce variance, which is why hyperscalers invest heavily in validated, pre-tuned stacks like Spectrum-X rather than hand-tuning DCQCN per deployment. Slingshot's per-flow congestion control tends to hold up well under mixed job traffic (multiple concurrent jobs sharing a Dragonfly fabric), a scenario where PFC-based fabrics are more exposed to head-of-line blocking. For all fabrics, NCCL's double-binary-tree algorithm gives near-logarithmic latency scaling with node count, but the constant factor — and how gracefully it degrades under contention — is exactly where the fabric choice shows up.

Worked Example: All-Reduce Time for 512 GPUs Across Three Fabrics

Consider a 512-GPU job performing a ring all-reduce on a 1 GB gradient tensor per step (64 nodes × 8 GPUs, one 400G-class rail per GPU). A simplified ring all-reduce completion-time model is T ≈ 2(N-1)/N × (S/B) + latency_term, where N is the number of participants, S is the message size, and B is the achievable per-link bandwidth.

InfiniBand NDR (400 Gb/s, SHARP-accelerated): with SHARP offloading roughly half the reduction traffic into the switch fabric, achievable effective bandwidth is high and consistent; a representative measured completion time for this pattern is on the order of ~1.8 ms per step at this scale.

RoCEv2 / Spectrum-X (400 Gb/s, well-tuned DCQCN): with a properly tuned fabric, achievable bandwidth approaches InfiniBand's, but the lack of in-network SHARP-style reduction and typically slightly higher tail latency under contention puts a representative completion time around ~2.3 ms per step — roughly 25-30% slower than the SHARP-accelerated InfiniBand case at the same scale.

Slingshot (200 Gb/s per direction, per-flow congestion control): the lower raw per-port rate is the dominant factor; a representative completion time lands around ~3.0 ms per step, though Slingshot's per-flow fairness means this number degrades more gracefully than a PFC-based fabric when multiple jobs share the network concurrently.

These are illustrative, not lab-measured, numbers — the point of the exercise is the shape of the trade-off: raw per-port bandwidth sets the floor, in-network compute (SHARP) can meaningfully beat that floor, and congestion-control architecture determines how much of that theoretical number you actually keep once the fabric is shared and busy.
